Explain the history and idea of \gls{QC}. Starting from~\citet{RN879}, and then explain the recent development of~\citet{blechschmidt:2017} and~\citet{blechschmidt_general_2020}. Mention specific works including~\citet{Cherubini_Coquand_Hutzler_2024},~\citet{,cherubini2024foundation}, and of course~\citet{sterling2025domains}.

Mention that from concrete applications, two directions of generalising \gls{QC} for finitary Horn theories are needed. One is to generalise it to \emph{infinitary}, or at least \emph{countable}, Horn theories. Note that the development for synthetic topology in~\citet{cherubini2024foundation} requires \gls{QC} for countably presented algebras, but still for a finitary algebraic theory, viz.\ the theory of Boolean algebras. However, the developments for \gls{SDT} in \citet{sterling2025domains} require to have a version of \gls{QC} for an infinitary Horn theory, viz.\ that of $\sigma$-frames (cf.~\zcref{exa:theoryofsigmaframe}). This will be developed in~\zcref{ch:sqchorn}.

Other applications also require us to generalise \gls{QC} to another \emph{type} of theories, viz.\ to \emph{coherent theories}. Through the process of Morleyisation (cf.~\citet[Prop.~2.1.1]{caramello2018theories}) every classical first-order theory can be understood as a coherent theory, thus indeed most structures we see in everyday mathematical life can be axiomatised this way. 

The reason a principle of \gls{QC} for coherent theories is desirable is that, \gls{QC} for Horn theories sometimes is not strong enough to prove the desired result that we expect to have synthetically. A simple example with the \emph{Sierpi\'nski topos} $\mc I$ is provided in~\zcref{sec:testcasesierpinski}. It is not hard to see that $\mc I$ classifies \emph{propositions}, and the generic proposition in $\mc I$ is \emph{dense} in the sense of~\zcref{def:propertiesofpropositions}, i.e.\ it is $\neg\neg$-closed (cf.~\zcref{prop:genericpropnegneg}). $\mc I$ is the classifying topos of both an algebraic theory and a coherent theory. However, \gls{QC} for the corresponding algebraic theory in $\mc I$ fails to establish the denseness of the generic proposition in $\mc I$ (cf.~\zcref{prop:algebraicnotsufficefordense}), while \gls{QC} for the corresponding coherent theory in $\mc I$ does suffice (cf.~\zcref{exa:qcinsierpinskitopos}). 

Of course, more importantly there are potentially lot more applications of \gls{QC} for coherent theories towards synthetic mathematics. We will come back to this in~\zcref{sec:logicandmath}.

\section{Quasi-coherence as an internal definability result}

Explain that, from a logical perspective, \gls{QC} is a definability result, i.e. maps between objects in the site are definable from the site. For this, first explain the result by~\citet{RN879}, which states that for a finitary Horn theory $\T$, functions on the generic model $V_\T$ in the classifying topos $\Set[\T]$ are all internally definable by terms,
\[ V_\T[\ms x] \cong V_{\T}^{V_\T}. \]

Recently, the work of Blass has been greatly generalised by Blechschmidt in his PhD thesis~\citep{blechschmidt:2017} (also see the unpublished note~\citep{blechschmidt_general_2020} by the same author), which identifies a stronger property satisfied by the generic model $V_\T$ in $\Set[\T]$, termed \emph{(synthetic) quasi-coherence (\gls{QC})}. The terminology is motivated by its application in algebraic geometry.

The main algebraic objects of study within the classifying topos $\Set[\T]$ will be the $V_\T$-algebras, i.e.\ $\T$-models $A$ equipped with a homomorphism $V_\T\to A$. Of course, the identity homomorphism exhibits $V_\T$ as a $V_\T$-algebra, and given any object $X$, we may define \[\opens X\coloneq V_\T^X\] to be the $X$-fold product $\prod_{x\in X}V_\T$ of $V_\T$-algebras. 

Conversely, given any $V_\T$-algebra $A$ we may define its \emph{spectrum} to be the type of $V_\T$-algebra homomorphisms 
\[ \spec A \coloneq V_\T\alg(A,V_\T)\text{.}\]

The principle of \gls{QC} in $\Set[\T]$ proposed by \citet{blechschmidt:2017} is, then, that for any \emph{finitely presented} $V_\T$-algebra $A$, the canonical homomorphism
\[
  A\to \opens\spec A
\]
Here mention that this can in fact be viewed as an internal duality result (cf.~\zcref{rem:ageneralduality}), and say something more here perhaps.

The appearance of finite presentation in the formulation of \gls{QC} above is perhaps misleading. Following the development of \citet{blechschmidt:2017,blechschmidt_general_2020}, it is at least conceivable that \gls{QC} with respect to finitely presented algebras can be generalised to larger cardinality, if we enlarge the site of the classifying topos $\Set[\T]$. For instance, one can consider
\[ \Set[\T_{\omega_1}] \coloneq [\mmod\T\cp,\Set]\text{,} \]
where $\mmod\T\cp$ denotes the category of \emph{countably presented} $\T$-models. In this case, \gls{QC} will hold also with respect to \emph{countably presented} $V_\T$-algebras. This is the theoretical basis in a recent approach for synthetic topology suggested by \citet{cherubini2024foundation}, who consider a variation on the quasi-coherence principle for \emph{countably} presented Boolean algebras. 

Horn theories $\T$ whose operations have \emph{countable} arity can also be considered in this case, so long as the base topos $\Set$ satisfies countable choice. These examples will not fit directly into the discipline of classifying toposes because geometric theories cannot have infinitary operations; however, we will discuss a notion of \emph{$\kappa$-topos} for a size $\kappa$, such that there will be a \emph{classifying $\kappa$-topos} for such theories (cf.~\zcref{sec:classifyingtopforhorn}).

Also include the external characterisation of the subobject classifier in a coherent topos and indicate how an axiomatisation for the universe of propositions might look like for a coherent theory. Also explain that, from a categorical or geometrical perspective, \gls{QC} (at least for finitary theories) is related to fibred Yoneda.

Explain that, at the end of the day, \gls{QC} says the syntactic category of an internal theory fully faithful embeds into the internal category of sets of the classifying topos, via evaluating at the identity on the generic model. Hence, how powerful or useful the \gls{QC} principle is depends a lot on how effective we can compute the syntactic category. Then naturally leads to~\zcref{sec:logicandmath}.

\section{On the connection of synthetic mathematics and logical theories}\label{sec:logicandmath}

Here explain that, beyond the technical development, the more important insight of this thesis is that there is a deeper connection between a particular style of synthetic mathematics for some field, and an algebraic/logical structure behind. This differs a lot from previous styles of synthetic mathematics based on realisability models, and is a new way of doing synthetic mathematics that may provide new insights. 

Explain that for algebraic/Horn theories, the structure of the logical theory is equivalent to how \emph{finite/$\kappa$-presented algebras} are computed. The concrete computation of such algebras then connects to the structure of the resulting classifying topos, thus connecting to synthetic mathematics.

For coherent theories, various classical techniques like \emph{quantifier elimination} allows us to compute the resulting syntactic category, and thus leading to structures in the classifying topos.

And one of the main contributions of this thesis is to exemplify this claim by supplying many (surprising) examples. In particular, the seemingly different structures of domain theory and distributive lattices and $\sigma$-frames (cf.~\zcref{ch:domain}), of computability theory and arithmetic (cf.~\zcref{ch:coharith}), and of probability theory and atomless Boolean algebras (cf.~\zcref{ch:BAandprobability}), are intimately tied together. The classifying topoi of the latter theories in all cases provide \emph{models} for the synthetic development of the former field of mathematics, and \gls{QC} introduces new techniques in reasoning about the synthetic mathematics happening this way.

Also explain our examples will justify the two types of generalisations of \gls{QC} that will be developed in~\zcref{ch:sqchorn,ch:sqccoh}.

\section{Foundation choices}\label{sec:foundation}

As mentioned, this document is going to contain two parts, where \zcref{prt:theory} works \emph{externally} from the semantics perspective and proves the validity of the quasi-coherence principle in a variety of topoi, and~\zcref{prt:appl} works \emph{internally} in various classifying topoi where we show the quasi-coherence principle for different theories have different applications in branches of synthetic mathematics.

However, in terms of the mathematical development within the two parts, especially the choice of the back ground logical system, they can be put on an equal footing, by working within a constructive meta-theory. More concretely, we work uniformly in the Bishop-style constructive mathematics, that can be interpreted in the internal language of \emph{any} topos. 

In addition to the usual constructive internal language of topoi, where every quantifier is assumed to be bounded by some small set, we also allow \emph{unbounded quantifiers}. Semantically, this is supported by the \emph{stack semantics} developed by~\citet{shulman2010stack}, which is an extension of the usual Kripke-Joyal semantics that can additionally supports the interpretation of unbounded quantifiers over all sets internally in a topos. Effectively, this means we have a category $\Set$ of (small) sets, and we can make sense of statements of the form: ``For any set $X\in\Set$, \ldots'' In~\zcref{prt:appl} and also in~\zcref{sec:internalduality} where we mainly work internally in a topos $\mc E$ different than the base topos $\Set$, we will also use the explicitly different notation $\mb S$ to refer to the category of sets internally in $\mc E$.

One reason we need a small dose of unbounded quantification in our development is to formulate constructively e.g.\ the notion of \emph{internal projectivity} in a topos. We thus also take the opportunity to mention that, throughout this document we will assume that \emph{countable choice} holds, i.e.\ the natural numbers object $\N$ is projective in the base category $\Set$ of sets. In many stream of constructive mathematics this is indeed considered as a valid principle, though its validity is also often debated in the literature; we refer the interested reader to the chapter by~\citet{richman_countable}. 

In $\Set$ there is also a universe of \emph{propositions}, which we denote as $\pp$. We will identify a proposition with its \emph{extension}, viz.\ as a subset of the terminal set $1$. We do assume $\pp$ is \emph{impredicative}, in the sense that quantifying over all sets still produces a proposition in the universe $\pp$. Internally in an arbitrary topos, this form of impredicativity is indeed supported by the stack semantics. Similar to the case of $\Set$, when we work within the internal language of a topos, we will often write the universe of propositions as $\Omega$, which is the standard notation for the subobject classifier in a topos.

Since we work in general in a constructive environment, propositions satisfying certain classical properties deserve additional attention:

\begin{definition}[Classical properties of propositions]\label{def:propertiesofpropositions}
  We say a proposition $p \in \pp$ is \emph{decidable}, if $p = 0 \vee p = 1$. We say $p$ is \emph{classical}, if $\neg\neg p = p$. We say $p$ is \emph{dense}, if $\neg\neg p = 1$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The subuniverse of decidable and classical propositions]
  Note that the set of decidable propositions is given by $2 = \set{0,1}$. We also denote the set of classical propositions as $\pp_{\dneg}$, and it is evident that there are inclusions $2 \subseteq \pp_{\dneg} \subseteq \pp$. These are both \emph{dominances} as in~\zcref{def:dominance}.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Decidable and classical sets]\label{def:decidableset}
  For a set $X$, we say it is \emph{decidable} (resp.\ \emph{classical}) if its equalities are decidable (resp.\ classical), i.e.\ for any $x,y\in X$, the proposition $x = y$ is decidable (resp.\ classical).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Caveat on complemented subsets]\label{rem:complementedsubtype}
  Given a set $X$, it is common in the literature to refer to $S$ as a \emph{decidable subset} of $X$ if, as a function $S \colon X \to \pp$, it factors through the decidable propositions $2 \subseteq \pp$. However, this might be misunderstood as the set $S$ itself is decidable as in~\zcref{def:decidableset}. Hence, we will always refer to such a subset as a \emph{complemented} subset.
\end{remark}


\section{Notation and terminology conventions}\label{sec:notation}

We will use fonts $\ms x,\ms y,\ms z,\cdots$ to denote \emph{formal variables}.

For any $n\in\N$, we also use $n$ to denote the finite cardinal containing exactly $n$ elements. In particular, $0$ and $1$ will be identified with the proposition \emph{falsum} and \emph{verum}, respectively.

For any set $X$, we write $\pss{X}$ for the proposition that $X$ is inhabited, viz.\ $\ex{x}{X}\top$.

We reserve symbols $\mc A,\mc B,\mc C$ for small categories. Fixed large categories are often denoted by bold face symbols, like $\Set$, $\Fin$, etc. We will reserve the sf font for 2-categories. For instance, $\Catt$ will denote the \emph{2-category} of categories, while $\Cat$ will be the \emph{1-category} of categories and functors. Other common 2-categories include $\Lex$, the 2-category of small lex categories, and $\Top$, the 2-category of topoi.

We use $\yon$ to denote the Yoneda embedding. More explicitly, we use the symbol for both the covariant and contravariant Yoneda embedding, but distinguishing them by subscription or superscription,
\[ \yon_- \colon \mc C \hook \psh(\mc C), \quad \yon^- \colon \mc C\op \hook [\mc C,\Set]. \]

In our constructive setting, by an \emph{equivalence} between categories we mean a functor $F \colon \mc A \to \mc B$ that is both \emph{fully faithful} and \emph{essentially surjective}. This is sometimes also referred to as a \emph{weak equivalence} between categories. For most practical usage, the notion of weak equivalence suffices, since one can show that all relevant categorical properties are preserved under weak equivalence.
